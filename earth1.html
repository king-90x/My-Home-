Then the human arrived.

For 4.5 billion years, Earth changed on its own schedule. Then one species learned to build, burn and multiply — and the schedule changed completely.

Where the acceleration began

The age of industry

For most of human history, our impact on Earth stayed local. That changed in the space of a few generations, once we learned to burn stored sunlight — coal, oil and gas — at industrial scale.

  • Industrial Revolution

    Beginning around 1760, machines began replacing human and animal labour.

  • Factories

    Mass production concentrated pollution at a scale never seen before.

  • Fossil fuels

    Coal, then oil and gas, became the engine of nearly everything built since.

What we put up there stays

The air changed

Carbon dioxide levels have risen faster in the last 170 years than in any period scientists can find in the ice-core record — and the atmosphere has responded exactly as physics predicted it would.

  • Carbon emissions

    Global CO₂ output has grown roughly fourfold since 1950.

  • Greenhouse gases

    CO₂, methane and others trap heat that would otherwise escape to space.

  • Air pollution

    Beyond warming, particulate pollution affects health in cities worldwide.

The planet's buffer is straining

The oceans changed

The ocean has quietly absorbed most of the extra heat and roughly a third of the extra carbon dioxide we've produced. That absorption comes at a cost: warmer water, more acidic chemistry, and a slow accumulation of plastic.

  • Ocean warming

    Average sea surface temperature has risen measurably since pre-industrial times.

  • Acidification

    Absorbed CO₂ is lowering ocean pH, stressing shell- and coral-building life.

  • Ocean pollution

    Plastic, runoff and waste reach even the most remote waters.

The planet's clearest gauge

The ice started moving

Glaciers are slow, patient recorders of climate — and nearly all of them, worldwide, are now retreating. What took centuries to build is melting within a human lifetime.

  • Glaciers

    The vast majority of the world's monitored glaciers are losing mass.

  • Polar ice

    Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk by roughly half since satellite records began.

  • Sea level

    Meltwater and warming seas have raised global sea level over 20 cm since 1900.

Cleared faster than it can regrow

Forests disappeared

Roughly a third of Earth's forests have been cleared since large-scale agriculture began, with the pace accelerating sharply over the last century — mostly for farmland, grazing and timber.

  • Deforestation

    Tens of thousands of square kilometres of forest are lost every year.

  • Habitat loss

    Clearing removes the homes of countless species at once, not just trees.

  • Forest fires

    Warmer, drier conditions are making wildfire seasons longer and harsher.

Deforestation in Rondônia, Brazil, photographed by NASA

Quietly, one by one

Species are vanishing

Extinction is a natural process — but the current rate is estimated at tens to hundreds of times the natural background rate, driven mainly by habitat loss, hunting and a changing climate.

  • Endangered species

    More than a quarter of assessed species face a meaningful risk of extinction.

  • Extinction

    Some losses, like the passenger pigeon, are already permanent.

  • Biodiversity loss

    Wildlife population sizes have fallen sharply on average since 1970.

A material that doesn't leave

The plastic planet

Humanity has produced more plastic in the last twenty years than in all the decades before that combined. Almost none of it has fully disappeared — it just gets smaller.

  • Plastic production

    Global output now exceeds 400 million tonnes a year.

  • Ocean plastic

    An estimated 8 million tonnes enters the ocean annually.

  • Microplastics

    Fragments now show up in rain, soil, and human blood.

Where most of us now live

The city planet

More than half of humanity now lives in cities, a milestone crossed only recently in our history. Concrete and asphalt trap heat, reshape rainfall, and are quietly becoming one of the planet's largest surfaces.

  • Urbanization

    The urban share of the population has more than doubled since 1950.

  • Heat islands

    Cities can run several degrees warmer than the countryside around them.

  • Green cities

    Parks and tree cover are increasingly treated as infrastructure, not decoration.

The part that's harder to see

The invisible footprint

Every meal, purchase and trip carries a hidden ledger — carbon released, water consumed, waste created. None of it shows up on the receipt, but all of it adds up somewhere.

  • Carbon footprint

    The greenhouse gas cost of a single life varies enormously by country.

  • Water footprint

    A single burger can represent well over a thousand litres of water.

  • Consumption & waste

    Global resource use has roughly tripled since 1970.

A thought experiment

Earth without us

If humans vanished tomorrow, how long would our mark actually last? The honest answer is: less than you'd think, and longer than you'd hope — depending entirely on what you're asking about.

  • Day 1

    Without maintenance, power and water systems begin failing almost immediately.

  • 100 years

    Most wooden and unmaintained structures have collapsed.

  • 1,000 years

    Forests have reclaimed the great majority of cities.

  • 1 million years

    Almost everything we built has eroded away or been buried.

What have we changed, and what can still be repaired?

The record so far is honest but not finished. Every system covered here — air, ocean, ice, forest, species — still responds to what we do next. The harder question isn't really about the planet at all.
