From the clouds above to the soil below, this is a journey through every layer of the world you already stand on — most of which you've never seen.

Where the journey begins

Above the clouds

Clouds are where the descent starts — visible weather, floating a few kilometres over your head, built from nothing more than water and temperature. Every storm, every clear day, starts up here.

  • Clouds

    Water vapour condensed around tiny particles of dust or salt.

  • Weather

    The constant churn of air, pressure and moisture that clouds make visible.

  • Atmosphere

    The thin envelope of gas that makes all of this possible.

Not one sky, but five

The sky has layers

Keep going up past the clouds and the sky changes character completely — thinner, colder, then stranger, until it isn't really "sky" anymore at all.

  • Troposphere

    0–12 km. Where all weather and breathable air live.

  • Stratosphere

    12–50 km. Home to the ozone layer and cruising aircraft.

  • Mesosphere

    50–85 km. Most meteors burn up before reaching further.

  • Thermosphere

    85–600 km. Auroras form here; the ISS orbits inside it.

  • Exosphere

    600 km and beyond, thinning until it becomes open space.

Now we go the other way

Into the ocean

Cross back down through the clouds, past the surface, and the descent continues — this time into water. The first 200 metres hold nearly all the colour and life most people ever associate with the sea.

  • Ocean zones

    Sunlight reaches only the top 200 metres of open water.

  • Marine life

    The overwhelming majority of ocean species live in this lit layer.

  • Coral ecosystems

    Reefs cover under 1% of the seafloor, yet shelter a quarter of marine species.

Coral outcrop, Flynn Reef — part of the Great Barrier Reef

Past where light gives up

The dark ocean

Below 1,000 metres, sunlight never arrives at all. Pressure climbs into the hundreds of atmospheres, temperatures hover near freezing, and yet — against every expectation — life persists.

  • Deep sea

    More than 90% of the ocean's volume lies in permanent darkness.

  • Extreme creatures

    Anglerfish, giant squid and tube worms thrive without ever seeing the sun.

  • Underwater ecosystem

    Many species make their own light — bioluminescence in place of daylight.

Back on land, looking up

Into the forest

A forest is its own vertical world — a canopy catching the light, an understory making do with what's left, and a floor doing the quiet work of breaking everything back down.

  • Forest layers

    Canopy, understory and floor, each with its own climate and residents.

  • Wildlife

    Forests hold roughly 80% of the world's documented land species.

  • Trees

    An estimated three trillion trees stand on Earth right now.

The world beneath the world

Under our feet

Soil looks like inert dirt from above. Underneath, it's a dense, living network — roots reaching for water, fungal threads trading nutrients between trees that have never touched.

  • Soil

    A living mixture of minerals, decayed matter, water and air.

  • Roots

    Anchor the plant and pull in water and nutrients from far below.

  • Fungi

    Mycorrhizal networks connect separate trees into a shared system.

Too small to see, too many to count

The hidden city

Zoom in far enough and soil stops looking empty. A single gram can hold billions of bacteria, along with fungi, archaea and microbes still being catalogued for the first time.

  • Microorganisms

    The most numerous, least visible residents of nearly every ecosystem.

  • Bacteria

    A single teaspoon of soil can outnumber the human population many times over.

  • Fungi

    Decomposers that quietly recycle nearly everything that dies.

A case study, up close

One tree

Zoom back out to a single tree, and everything you've just walked through comes together at once — birds in its branches, insects in its bark, fungi at its roots, and people relying on all of it.

  • Tree ecosystem

    One mature tree can host thousands of individual organisms.

  • Birds & insects

    Nest, feed and pollinate, often depending on that one tree alone.

  • Soil & humans

    Roots feed the soil; people rely on the shade, fruit, wood and air.

Follow it all the way

One drop

Trace a single drop of water and you retrace this entire journey — lifted from the ocean, folded into a cloud, released as rain, carried by a river, and eventually, drunk by you.

  • Ocean → Cloud

    Evaporated by the Sun and lifted into the sky as vapour.

  • Rain → River

    Falls back down and gathers into streams heading for the sea.

  • Human

    Roughly 60% of your body is the same water, on loan.

The exchange you never notice

One breath

Every breath you take is half of a trade that's been running for billions of years — plants releasing the oxygen you need, and quietly taking back the carbon dioxide you don't.

  • Oxygen cycle

    Produced by plants and plankton, absorbed by nearly everything else.

  • Carbon cycle

    Carbon dioxide exhaled by animals becomes fuel for plant growth.

  • Plants ↔ Humans

    A quiet, constant exchange neither side could survive without.

Every layer you just walked through is connected.

The clouds feed the rivers. The rivers feed the roots. The roots feed the fungi, the fungi feed the trees, and the trees feed the air you're breathing right now. Pull on any single thread and the whole web moves.

Nothing exists alone.
