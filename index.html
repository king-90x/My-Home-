A biography, briefly

4.5 billion years

Earth is 4.54 billion years old, give or take fifty million — a figure worked out from the oldest meteorites in the solar system, which formed alongside the planet itself. Almost everything that has ever happened here fits inside that span.

  • Formation

    Built from dust and debris circling the young Sun, 4.54 billion years ago.

  • First oceans

    Liquid water appears roughly 4 billion years ago, astonishingly early.

  • First life

    Simple microbes take hold by 3.8 billion years ago, maybe earlier.

Before it had a colour

Before blue

The young Earth was nothing like the planet in your photographs. It was a scarred, molten world without oxygen, without oceans, and without a single living thing — and it stayed that way for hundreds of millions of years.

  • Early Earth

    A hostile, volcanic surface under constant bombardment from space debris.

  • Formation

    A Mars-sized body likely collided with Earth, throwing off debris that became the Moon.

  • Turning blue

    Water carried by comets and asteroids slowly filled the first ocean basins.

Earthrise, Apollo 8 — 24 December 1968

Nothing here stands still

The planet that moves

You are currently spinning at roughly 1,670 kilometres an hour and hurtling around the Sun at over 107,000 kilometres an hour — and you feel none of it. Earth's motion is what gives us day, night and the seasons.

  • Rotation

    One spin on its axis every 23 hours, 56 minutes.

  • Revolution

    One full orbit of the Sun every 365.25 days.

  • Day & night

    Caused by rotation, not by the Sun moving around us.

  • Seasons

    A 23.5° axial tilt, not distance from the Sun, is what drives them.

What's underfoot, going down

The heart of Earth

Cut the planet in half and you'd find four distinct layers, each behaving completely differently — from rock that flows like tar over millions of years to a metal core hotter than the surface of the Sun.

  • Inner core

    Solid iron and nickel, kept solid by crushing pressure despite the heat.

  • Outer core

    Molten metal motion, generating the planet's magnetic field.

  • Mantle

    Hot, slow-moving rock that makes up most of Earth's volume.

A shell in constant motion

The skin of Earth

The crust is broken into roughly fifteen major plates, drifting a few centimetres a year — about the speed your fingernails grow. Where they meet, they build mountains, open oceans and occasionally erupt.

  • Continents

    Seven landmasses, still drifting apart at a barely perceptible pace.

  • Tectonic plates

    Rigid slabs of crust that float on the softer mantle beneath.

  • Mountains

    Raised where plates collide and crumple the crust upward.

  • Volcanoes

    Around 1,500 potentially active volcanoes exist on land today.

The air above you

The breath

The atmosphere is thin — 90% of it sits within just 16 kilometres of the surface — but it does enormous work: holding in warmth, blocking radiation, and giving every living thing something to breathe.

  • Nitrogen

    About 78% of the air, mostly inert and easy to overlook.

  • Oxygen

    Roughly 21%, produced almost entirely by living things.

  • Layers

    Five bands, from the weather-filled troposphere to the open exosphere.

What moves it all

The bloodstream

Water covers 71% of the surface and never stops moving — evaporating from oceans, falling as rain, carving rivers, and finding its way, eventually, back to the sea. The same molecules have been cycling for billions of years.

  • Oceans

    Five oceans holding roughly 97% of the planet's water.

  • Rivers & lakes

    The freshwater arteries that carry water back toward the sea.

  • Water cycle

    A closed loop of evaporation, condensation and precipitation, powered by the Sun.

The planet's engine room

The green machine

Every breath you take passed through a leaf first. Forests and ocean plankton quietly convert sunlight into the oxygen that fills the air and the carbon that builds every living cell.

  • Forests

    Covering roughly 30% of Earth's land surface.

  • Photosynthesis

    Light, water and carbon dioxide converted into sugar and oxygen.

  • Carbon cycle

    Carbon moves constantly between air, ocean, soil and living things.

Seen from far enough away

Earth from space

Fewer than seven hundred people have ever seen Earth whole, hanging against the black. Many describe the same jolt — a sudden, physical understanding that borders are invisible, and that everyone they know lives on one small, shared surface.

  • Astronaut view

    Often called the overview effect — a shift in perspective reported by those who've seen it.

  • Blue Marble

    The 1972 Apollo 17 photograph that became one of the most reproduced images ever taken.

  • Human perspective

    A vantage point almost no one in history has ever had access to.

One planet, against everything else there is.

From nearly four billion miles away, Voyager 1 turned its camera back toward home. Earth appeared as a single pale point of light — smaller than a pixel, lost in a sunbeam. Every person who has ever lived did so on that point.
